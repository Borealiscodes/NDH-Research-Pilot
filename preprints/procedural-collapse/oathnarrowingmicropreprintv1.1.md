Oath‑Taking, Procedural Narrowing, and Humane‑Process Collapse in Irish Tribunal Practice
Version: Research‑Pilot Micro‑Preprint v1.1  
Author: Borealis Serenity Hedling  
Location: Dublin, Ireland  
Date: October 2026  

---

Abstract

This micro‑preprint examines a procedural anomaly emerging within Irish quasi‑judicial tribunals: the narrowing of religious‑text availability and the informal practice of bypassing sworn oaths without statutory authority. Irish law requires sworn or affirmed evidence, and tribunals possess no discretion to waive this requirement. Recent cases demonstrate a pattern where appellants are unable to access their religious text for oath‑taking due to State‑level availability constraints, institutional refusals, and gaps in psychosocial support. This preprint documents the issue and situates it within broader research on humane‑process collapse in bureaucratically entrenched systems.

---

1. Introduction

In 2026, multiple appellants reported procedural irregularities in tribunal hearings, including:

- the absence of an administered oath or affirmation,  
- narrowing of religious texts available for oath‑taking,  
- reliance on “preferred availability” rather than statutory accommodation,  
- and inconsistent explanations regarding procurement of requested texts.

These practices are not supported by Irish law and raise concerns about procedural fairness, evidentiary validity, and the structural integrity of tribunal determinations.

This micro‑preprint provides a concise academic framing of these issues.

---

2. Statutory Framework

Irish law provides no mechanism for tribunals to waive sworn evidence.

Relevant statutory anchors include:

- Oaths Act 1909  
- Interpretation Act 2005  
- Civil Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 2008  
- Constitutional fair‑procedures doctrine  
- Tribunal of Inquiry jurisprudence

These establish:

1. Evidence must be sworn or affirmed.  
2. Appellants may swear on a religious text of their choosing.  
3. Tribunals must facilitate this unless impossible.  
4. Unsworn testimony cannot form the basis of a lawful determination.  

New tribunals such as TARA (established June 2026) inherit these constraints and cannot create discretionary exceptions.

---

3. Observed Procedural Narrowing

Unlike substitution — which did not occur — tribunals increasingly rely on preferred availability, meaning:

- only certain religious texts are routinely stocked,  
- requests outside this narrow set are not actioned,  
- procurement is treated as optional rather than mandatory,  
- and appellants are effectively limited to State‑preferred texts.

This narrowing is not grounded in statute and represents a form of administrative compression that restricts religious accommodation.

---

4. Case‑Specific Context: The Bhagavad Gita Request

Since 5 May 2026, Appellant Borealis Serenity Hedling requested access to the Bhagavad Gita for oath‑taking.

The following constraints were documented:

- Dublin City Libraries confirmed they could not source the text by the required date.  
- The Irish Government does not provide Article 14 UN CAT compensation, leaving the appellant unable to independently purchase the text.  
- SPIRASI declined psychosocial support when asked to waive GP referral requirements, despite the appellant’s history of punitive psychiatry, making attendance at the ISKCON temple (the only accessible source) impossible.  
- IPAT did not attempt procurement and relied on “preferred availability” rather than statutory accommodation.

These factors collectively prevented the appellant from accessing the religious text required for lawful oath‑taking.

---

5. Implications for Procedural Integrity

The inability to access a religious text for oath‑taking has several consequences:

- Evidentiary instability: Unsworn testimony cannot support credibility findings.  
- Determination vulnerability: Decisions based on unsworn evidence risk nullity.  
- Religious‑accommodation compression: State‑preferred availability replaces statutory rights.  
- Humane‑process collapse: Bureaucratic constraints override procedural fairness.  
- Structural drift: Tribunals operate outside statutory boundaries under administrative pressure.

These issues reflect systemic strain rather than isolated error.

---

6. Research Implications

Three emerging research pathways are identified:

1. Administrative Narrowing of Religious Accommodation  
   How “preferred availability” becomes a de facto policy without statutory basis.

2. Impact of Social‑Service Gaps on Procedural Rights  
   How lack of psychosocial supports (e.g., SPIRASI referral barriers) affects tribunal access.

3. Nullity Risk in Tribunal Determinations  
   How oath‑bypass practices undermine legal validity.

These pathways support broader work on humane‑process collapse in bureaucratically entrenched systems.

---

7. Conclusion

The narrowing of religious‑text availability and the informal bypassing of oath‑taking requirements represent a measurable procedural defect in Irish tribunal practice. These practices lack statutory foundation and emerge from administrative constraints, service gaps, and institutional refusals rather than legal authority. This micro‑preprint documents the issue and situates it within ongoing research into humane‑process collapse.
