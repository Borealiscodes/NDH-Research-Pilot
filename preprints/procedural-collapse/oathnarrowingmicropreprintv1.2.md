Abstract

This micro‑preprint examines a procedural anomaly emerging within Irish quasi‑judicial tribunals: the narrowing of religious‑text availability and the informal bypassing of sworn oaths without statutory authority. External reports from the Irish Refugee Council, UNHCR, ECRE’s AIDA project, IHREC, and academic literature corroborate broader patterns of procedural inconsistency within the International Protection Appeals Tribunal (IPAT). Recent case‑specific constraints—including library unavailability, lack of Article 14 UN CAT compensation, and psychosocial‑support barriers—further illustrate how systemic gaps can prevent appellants from accessing the religious text required for lawful oath‑taking. This preprint situates these observations within the wider research landscape on humane‑process collapse in bureaucratically entrenched systems.

---

1. Introduction

In 2026, appellants reported procedural irregularities in tribunal hearings, including:

- absence of administered oaths or affirmations,  
- narrowing of religious texts available for oath‑taking,  
- reliance on “preferred availability” rather than statutory accommodation,  
- inconsistent explanations regarding procurement of requested texts.

These observations align with external analyses documenting variability, inconsistency, and procedural drift within IPAT.  
Reports from the Irish Refugee Council (IRC), UNHCR, ECRE’s AIDA project, and IHREC all note irregularities in hearing conduct, credibility assessment, and procedural safeguards.

This micro‑preprint provides a concise academic framing of these issues.

---

2. Statutory Framework

Irish law provides no mechanism for tribunals to waive sworn evidence.

Relevant statutory anchors include:

- Oaths Act 1909  
- Interpretation Act 2005  
- Civil Law (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 2008  
- Constitutional fair‑procedures doctrine  
- Tribunal of Inquiry jurisprudence

These establish:

1. Evidence must be sworn or affirmed.  
2. Appellants may swear on a religious text of their choosing.  
3. Tribunals must facilitate this unless impossible.  
4. Unsworn testimony cannot form the basis of a lawful determination.

External bodies—including UNHCR and IHREC—have repeatedly emphasized the importance of consistent procedural safeguards in protection appeals.  
New tribunals such as TARA (established June 2026) inherit these constraints and cannot create discretionary exceptions.

3. Observed Procedural Narrowing

Unlike substitution—which did not occur—tribunals increasingly rely on preferred availability, meaning:

- only certain religious texts are routinely stocked,  
- requests outside this narrow set are not actioned,  
- procurement is treated as optional rather than mandatory,  
- appellants are effectively limited to State‑preferred texts.

This narrowing mirrors patterns documented in:

- IRC’s “Person or Number?” (2021), which highlights inconsistent procedural safeguards;  
- AIDA Country Reports (2022–2024), which note variability in hearing practices and lack of standardisation;  
- IHREC submissions, which identify procedural deficiencies and unclear internal guidance.

These external sources confirm that procedural drift is a known systemic issue, even if oath‑specific irregularities are not explicitly documented.

4. Case‑Specific Context: The Bhagavad Gita Request

Since 5 May 2026, Appellant Borealis Serenity Hedling requested access to the Bhagavad Gita for oath‑taking.

Documented constraints include:

- Dublin City Libraries confirmed they could not source the text by the required date.  
- The Irish Government does not provide Article 14 UN CAT compensation, preventing independent procurement.  
- SPIRASI declined psychosocial support when asked to waive GP referral requirements, despite the appellant’s history of punitive psychiatry, making attendance at the ISKCON temple impossible.  
- IPAT relied on “preferred availability” rather than statutory accommodation.

These constraints illustrate how service gaps, resource limitations, and institutional refusals can collectively prevent appellants from accessing the religious text required for lawful oath‑taking.

5. External Corroboration of Procedural Irregularities

Multiple independent bodies have documented systemic irregularities within IPAT:

- Irish Refugee Council (IRC) reports “significant variability in hearing conduct” and “inconsistent procedural safeguards.”  
- UNHCR Ireland notes “variability in the conduct of interviews and hearings” and “procedural gaps affecting fairness.”  
- ECRE’s AIDA Reports identify “inconsistencies in the appeals process” and “lack of standardisation in hearing practices.”  
- IHREC highlights “procedural deficiencies” and “lack of clarity in internal tribunal guidance.”  
- Academic literature (Thornton, Costello) discusses administrative drift, evidentiary irregularities, and structural defects in protection appeals.

These sources collectively demonstrate that the procedural anomalies documented in this preprint are part of a broader, externally recognized pattern.

---

6. Implications for Procedural Integrity

The inability to access a religious text for oath‑taking has several consequences:

- Evidentiary instability: Unsworn testimony cannot support credibility findings.  
- Determination vulnerability: Decisions based on unsworn evidence risk nullity.  
- Religious‑accommodation compression: State‑preferred availability replaces statutory rights.  
- Humane‑process collapse: Bureaucratic constraints override procedural fairness.  
- Structural drift: Tribunals operate outside statutory boundaries under administrative pressure.

External reports corroborate that IPAT already exhibits procedural variability, making oath‑related anomalies particularly concerning.

---

7. Conclusion

The narrowing of religious‑text availability and informal bypassing of oath‑taking requirements represent a measurable procedural defect in Irish tribunal practice. External sources confirm that IPAT already exhibits systemic irregularities, making these observations part of a broader pattern of administrative drift. This micro‑preprint documents the issue and situates it within ongoing research into humane‑process collapse.
